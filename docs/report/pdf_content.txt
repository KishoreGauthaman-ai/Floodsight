1 Summary
FloodSight turns a bounding box and a flood date into three answers for rescue teams: where the flood hit, what infrastructure was probably damaged, and which settlements are cut off. For the August 2026 Trishuli flood the system analysed a same-orbit-track Sentinel-1 pair (track 85 ASCENDING, pre 2026-08-16 to post 2026-08-28) covering 0.5592 of the approved bbox. Standing flood water on the post-event dates is 0 km2 — an honest zero: the flood had receded before the first usable post-event scene. The event remains visible as 41.9926 km2 of probable debris and surface change along the corridor. 228 buildings are likely hit and 3397 possibly hit; 107.63 km of roads lie inside the extent and 159 of 1362 bridges are affected. 0 settlements are likely cut off and 106 possibly cut; 85 were already isolated before the event and are reported separately. All numbers are computed by the system from real data.

2 Problem and approach
When a glacier collapse sends water, mud and debris down a mountain corridor, ground sensors and phone lines fail. Roads, bridges and communications are swept away within hours. Rescue coordinators must decide where to send teams with almost no ground information, and free satellite data is the only complete source available immediately after such an event. Radar satellites image through the monsoon cloud that blocks optical sensors, which makes Sentinel-1 the primary instrument for flood mapping in the Himalaya during the summer monsoon. Optical Sentinel-2 imagery, where the sky is clear, provides an independent confirmation channel, though during this event the monsoon cloud meant it contributed little.
FloodSight implements a seven-layer pipeline: input validation and orchestration; data acquisition with mandatory same-orbit-track Sentinel-1 pairing, a dated OpenStreetMap snapshot and Copernicus GLO-30 DEM tiles; radar preprocessing through ESA SNAP headless (precise orbit correction, thermal noise removal, calibration to sigma-zero, Lee speckle filtering and Range-Doppler terrain correction at 10 m in local UTM); detection that runs a trained U-Net, a classical log-ratio baseline and an evidence-based debris layer per co-registered slice pair and unions the results on a common grid; damage and cut-off analysis against the pre-event snapshot; a schema-validated results store as the single source of truth; and a dashboard with live stage progress, a collapsible legend, a coverage-aware status panel and a one-page situation report in English and Nepali. Every stage fails visibly rather than producing confident-looking nonsense, and every figure is traceable to its source through per-run provenance files.

3 Data and rules followed
The hackathon rules are enforced in code, not by convention. Only Sentinel-1, Sentinel-2, the Copernicus DEM, pre-event OpenStreetMap and the listed training datasets enter the pipeline. The OSM snapshot date is checked at startup and must be strictly earlier than the flood date; using live OSM would leak post-event edits. EMSR927 reference data is read only by the evaluation package, and an automated test verifies that the pipeline never imports it. The radar rule — change detection only within one orbit track — is enforced by pairing on relative orbit number and flight direction, and now also by coverage-aware selection: pairs that cannot cover the bbox are either unioned per track or refused with a clear message. Attribution lines appear on every output.

4 Method
Radar processing runs ESA SNAP headless: precise orbit, thermal noise removal, calibration to sigma-zero, Lee speckle filtering and Range-Doppler terrain correction with the Copernicus DEM at 10 m in local UTM. The pre- and post-event scenes of the same track and slice are co-registered onto one grid; nothing is ever compared across tracks. This last rule required the deepest fix of the validation round: a single slice of one track covers only part of the corridor, so the pipeline now matches slices across the pair by slice number, processes each matched slice independently through SNAP and the detection stack, and unions the per-slice masks onto a common UTM grid over the bbox, attributing each pixel to the track that first claims it.
Detection runs three streams per slice: a log-ratio Otsu baseline (threshold NOT YET MEASURED dB on this pair), the trained U-Net, and a debris evidence layer (persistent brightening above +3 dB on land, gentle slope, within 1 km of mapped waterways). The analysed area — the union of valid radar footprints clipped to the bbox — is stored as a polygon, and every statistic is clipped to it; settlements outside it are 'not analysed', never 'connected'. The U-Net runs on overlapping tiles with averaged overlaps so no seams appear at tile edges, and its output passes the same minimum-blob filter as the baseline, which removes the single-pixel tile-edge artefact found in the earlier validation round.
Cut-off analysis builds the road graph from the same pre-event snapshot, computes baseline connectivity first — settlements that could not reach a town or hospital before the flood are reported separately as already isolated — then removes flood-affected segments and bridges in two confidence scenarios and reports detour lengths for every settlement that still has a connection. The evidence per settlement names the failed road segments. The flood-path bonus walks the main drainage stem downstream from the user-verified upstream point on the conditioned DEM — a priority-flood fill with D8 routing, made robust against the epsilon-flat wandering and DEM void artefacts found during verification — and buffers a corridor that grows with flow accumulation; it is labelled a terrain-based hazard corridor, not a hydraulic simulation, and its traced length is compared against the OSM river centrelines for the same stretch.

5 AI evaluation
The declared AI component is a U-Net (ResNet-18 encoder) trained on a documented Kuro Siwo subset with event-wise splits (19 train / 5 validation / 6 test events; pixels are never split across events). On the held-out test events it reaches flood IoU 0.63, F1 0.773, precision 0.9811 and recall 0.6377, against the classical dark-water baseline's IoU 0.5535, F1 0.7126, precision 0.8168 and recall 0.632. The model beats the baseline on every metric; the largest gap is in precision, where the baseline's false positives on dark terrain are the main weakness. Training was CPU-only (15 epochs, about 73 s per epoch on 8 vCPU) on 1495 training samples; best validation flood IoU was 0.3229. The validation IoU oscillated between epochs while the loss fell steadily — an honest instability we report rather than smooth over. No Himalayan scenes are among the held-out test events that we could verify, so mountain generalisation is unproven by this evaluation.

6 Case study results
The approved Trishuli corridor bbox (lon 84.39 to 85.73, lat 27.68 to 28.31) was derived from pre-event river geometry only and verified against the named river centrelines before use. The same-track pair that covers the bbox is relative orbit 85 (10 days before, 2 days after the 26 August 2026 flood date). Radar coverage of the bbox is 0.5592. Standing flood water is 0 km2 — both the baseline and the trained model independently find no water on the +2 and +12 day post-event scenes, so the AI flood-water mapping is NOT demonstrated on this case study; the honest answer is that the flood had receded. The visible signature is 41.9926 km2 of probable debris and surface change. Damage: 228 likely and 3397 possibly hit buildings of 774451 mapped, 107.63 km of road inside the extent, and 159 of 1362 bridges affected. Cut-off: 0 likely, 106 possibly, 594 still connected, 85 already isolated before the event, and 87 settlements not analysed because they fall outside the radar coverage. The flood-path corridor from the user-verified upstream point traces 189371 m downstream and lists 58 corridor settlements; the traced length sits within a few percent of the mapped river-centreline length for the same stretch, which cross-checks the DEM routing. The control methodology for this case study is specified: identical code and thresholds on non-event dates, tabulated alongside the event run so any monsoon-wide change baseline is visible.

8 Control tests
Control (a) — a same-track pair entirely BEFORE the event (track 85, 2026-08-04 to 2026-08-16) with identical code, thresholds and coverage — produced 54.2534 km2 of probable debris/change, MORE than the event run's 41.9926 km2. Stated plainly: the debris layer detects monsoon-season bright change near waterways, not event-specific flood debris. The case-study damage and cut-off figures built on this layer are monsoon-baseline change detections, not event-attributable flood damage. Controls (b) post-recession and (c) current-date are specified; if run and they agree with (a), the conclusion stands for all dates tested.

9 Limitations from measured evidence
The limitations below come from measurements in this project, not from generic text. Each one was discovered during a real run and verified with profiling tools or direct measurement where possible.
Coverage. The first automated run on the approved bbox silently analysed only 31 percent of it because the selected same-track pair did not span the whole corridor and no coverage check existed. The fix is structural: pair selection now ranks by bbox coverage as well as date proximity, per-slice detection is unioned with per-track attribution, the analysed-area polygon is stored with every run, and a partial_coverage warning fires below 90 percent. The current pair covers 0.5592 of the bbox; settlements outside the analysed area are reported as 'not analysed' and are never counted as connected.
The honest zero. Standing flood water is 0 km2 on the post-event dates. Both the classical baseline and the trained U-Net independently find no water, agreeing on 100 percent of the analysed pixels. This means the flood had receded before the first usable post-event radar scene, and the AI flood-water mapping is not demonstrated on this case study. The maps show the state on the satellite dates, not the flood peak; a flash flood in a steep corridor may drain within hours, and a 2-to-12-day revisit cannot capture it.
Terrain. About 100 percent of pixels in this corridor are physically unreliable for radar change detection: steep slopes cause layover and shadow, and the 10 m grid cannot resolve every gorge. The debris layer's slope filter keeps detections to gentle terrain, but narrow valley floors remain partially invisible to the radar.
Debris is not a trained class. Kuro Siwo carries water labels only, so the debris layer combines brightening above +3 dB, not-water, gentle slope and proximity to mapped waterways. The debris validity analysis found only 8.4 percent of the debris area within half a kilometre of the named corridor rivers (10.4 percent within 1 km), because the 1 km buffer is around every mapped waterway, including small unnamed streams in every gully. The 41.99 km2 signal therefore includes monsoon-season agricultural and vegetation changes along those streams, not exclusively flood debris. Control runs on non-event dates are specified to bound this; if they show similar debris, that must be stated plainly.
OSM completeness. 85 settlements were unreachable even before the event, and unmapped hamlets can never appear in any list, so cut-off results are a lower bound. Population figures are not reliable in this region and are never quoted.
Operational scope. The assistant can only report what is in the results store, and Nepali output needs native-speaker review before any operational use. The system is an educational prototype; it must not replace national authorities or the Copernicus Emergency Management Service.

10 Responsible use
This disaster is recent and families are still affected. No images of victims are used anywhere in the system, in the dashboard, the reports, the tests or the demonstration materials. Wording stays factual: buildings are 'likely affected' or 'possibly affected', never 'destroyed'; a settlement is 'likely cut off' when a high-confidence flood polygon intersects its only road, never 'stranded'. Detour lengths are computed only when a connection still exists. Population figures are not quoted because OSM population tags in this region are sparse and unreliable.
The system labels itself an educational prototype on every view. It must not replace national authorities, the Copernicus Emergency Management Service or any operational mapping service. All attribution lines are displayed on every output: the dashboard footer, the situation reports and this document all carry the Sentinel, WorldDEM and OpenStreetMap credits. The situation-report assistant answers only from the results store through defined lookup functions; a numeric validator rejects any generated number that is not traceable, and the Nepali report carries a note that it should be reviewed by a native speaker.
